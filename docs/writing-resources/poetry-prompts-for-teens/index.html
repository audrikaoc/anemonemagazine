Poetry Prompts for Teens

Eight short exercises for writing with sound, line breaks, and unexpected details.

Pick one prompt, set a timer for ten minutes, and write a rough draft. If you get stuck, describe one thing you can see, hear, or touch. You can decide where to break the lines after you have words on the page.

  1. Write a place using sound

    Describe somewhere you know using four sounds. Start with the quietest sound and end with the loudest. Read the poem aloud and change one word so the line sounds more like the place.

  2. Leave the room

    Picture a room just after everyone leaves. Let three objects report what happened. Give each object a different opinion about the same moment.

  3. Look twice at one object

    Choose something you could fit in a pocket. Write four lines from the point of view of someone who lost it, then four from someone who finds it. Give both speakers the same final word.

  4. Change where a line ends

    Write a six-line poem about missing a bus. End one line with a word that could mean two things. Move that word to the next line and read both versions aloud. Keep the break that creates the stronger surprise.

  5. Make a map from memory

    Give someone directions to a familiar place using details you remember. Replace street names with clues such as the crooked fence or the smell outside a bakery. Let the final direction reveal why the place matters.

  6. Let a list change its mind

    Begin with a list of things you would pack for one ordinary day. Halfway through, let something happen that changes what the speaker needs. Continue the list and let the new objects tell the story.

  7. Use one sentence twice

    Write a three-line stanza that ends with “I’ll be there.” Write another three-line stanza that ends the same way. Change who is speaking or what has happened so the repeated sentence feels different.

  8. Ask the moon a question

    Ask the moon a question you would hesitate to ask a person. Give it an answer made of two images, then write one more line in your own voice.

Try one revision

Circle the line that feels most alive. Try starting the poem there. Read it aloud to find where your voice pauses, then decide which of those pauses should become line breaks.

Bring a draft to a free Anemone workshop or try the revision guide on a longer piece.
